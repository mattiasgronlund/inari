# Changelog

Items marked with ⚠️ are breaking changes.

## Unreleased

### Added

- Added support for architectures other than x86-64 and AArch64, such as wasm32 and riscv64, with a portable backend that does not change the rounding mode.
- Added 32-bit x86 Linux (`i686-unknown-linux-gnu`) and x86-64 Windows with the MSVC toolchain to the targets that CI tests. CI runs the unit and integration tests there, but not those that compare with MPFR, which are compiled only on 64-bit Linux and macOS.
- Added the Cargo feature `force-generic`, which uses the portable backend on x86-64 and AArch64 too, and on x86-64 keeps the multi-precision kernel from detecting the CPU at run time.
- Added a backend for WebAssembly that uses 128-bit SIMD instructions, enabled by the target feature `simd128`. It makes most interval operations other than multiplication 1.6 to 1.9 times as fast as the portable backend, and multiplication 1.3 to 1.5 times as fast.
- Added a backend for x86-64 processors that have SSE4.2 but neither AVX nor FMA, which lowers the lowest supported processor from Haswell to Nehalem. Like the WebAssembly backend, it does not change the rounding mode. On such a processor, it makes most interval operations 1.6 to 2.5 times as fast as the portable backend.
- Added correctly rounded `f64` functions in the module `inari::cr`: `exp`, `exp2`, `exp10`, `ln`, `log2`, `log10`, `sin`, `cos`, `tan`, `asin`, `acos`, `atan`, `atan2`, `sinh`, `cosh`, `tanh`, `asinh`, `acosh`, `atanh`, `pow` and `powi`, each rounded to nearest with ties to even, toward -∞ and toward +∞, such as `cr::exp_rn`, `cr::exp_rd` and `cr::exp_ru`. They give MPFR's values at the zeros, at the infinities and outside the domains, and `pow` gives those of C99 and MPFR at a finite x < 0 and at x = -∞. Each settles some arguments by rules, and the others by Ziv's strategy with enclosures of up to 4,096 bits. At an argument that reaches that cap with no rounding test passing, a function returns the rounding of a bound of the last enclosure, which is not known to be correctly rounded, but none of the 9,949,560 hard-to-round arguments that CORE-MATH lists for these functions reaches it. Tests on 64-bit Linux and macOS compare them with MPFR in all three roundings, at a sample of those arguments among others.
- Added correctly rounded `cbrt`, `hypot` and `sqrt` to `inari::cr`, each rounded to nearest with ties to even, toward -∞ and toward +∞, such as `cr::cbrt_rn`, `cr::hypot_rd` and `cr::sqrt_ru`. `cbrt` and `hypot` first try a fast path in `f64`, proved in Rocq to return the result rounded wherever it returns one, and only where it does not decide the rounding compute an exact integer cube or square root with the kernel, without Ziv's strategy and so with no cap. They give MPFR's values at the zeros and the infinities, including `hypot(±∞, y) = +∞` for a NaN `y`. `sqrt_rn` is `f64::sqrt`, and `sqrt_rd` and `sqrt_ru` are those of the backend of the interval arithmetic. Tests on 64-bit Linux and macOS compare them with MPFR in all three roundings, and `cbrt` and `hypot` at a sample of the hard-to-round arguments that CORE-MATH lists for them. On x uniform in [-10, 10), and on x of a random sign with a uniformly random exponent from -1074 to 1023 and significand, a call of `cbrt` of `cr` takes 1.4 to 1.5 times as many instructions as the correctly rounded `cbrt` of CORE-MATH in the same rounding, as counted with cachegrind. A call of `hypot` takes 1.4 to 1.5 times as many as that of CORE-MATH on x and y uniform in [-10, 10), and 1.9 to 2.2 times as many on x and y of random signs with uniformly random exponents from -1074 to 1023 and significands, drawn again until their binary exponents differ by 27 or more, where it computes a square root only for a few pairs with a subnormal argument. Arguments that the fast path declines, such as exact results rounded toward -∞ or +∞ (`cr::cbrt_rd(8.0)`, `cr::hypot_ru(3.0, 4.0)`), results of `hypot` midway between two `f64`s rounded to nearest, and `hypot` of two subnormals, still take the kernel's path: on exact cubes and on scaled Pythagorean pairs, rounded toward -∞ or +∞, a call of `cbrt` takes 5.6 to 5.7 times as many instructions as that of CORE-MATH, and one of `hypot` 15 times as many.
- Added `Interval::cbrt`, `Interval::hypot`, `DecInterval::cbrt` and `DecInterval::hypot`, IEEE 1788's recommended functions rootn(x, 3) and hypot(x, y), which compute their bounds with the functions of the kernel that `cr::cbrt_rd`, `cr::cbrt_ru`, `cr::hypot_rd` and `cr::hypot_ru` call, and return the tightest interval at every argument. `hypot` of a bounded box can overflow, as in `hypot([f64::MAX, f64::MAX], [f64::MAX, f64::MAX])` = [`f64::MAX`, +∞], which `DecInterval::hypot` decorates `dac`, as IEEE 1788 asks.

### Changed

- ⚠️ Bumped the minimum supported Rust version to 1.86.0.
- Made the default backend for x86-64 processors with AVX and FMA compute the directed roundings explicitly, instead of switching the rounding mode around every instruction. No backend for x86-64 changes the rounding mode any more. On a Zen 2 processor, interval addition and subtraction take 0.47 times as long as before, multiplication 0.49 to 0.62 (0.78 to 0.79 where the products are below 2^-968), division 0.67 to 0.69, evaluating a degree-8 polynomial 0.41 to 0.45, and a 3×3 determinant 0.33. Only the latency of a chain of dependent `add_ru`, `div_ru` and `mul_add_ru` operations became slightly worse, by 3 to 12 percent.
- Made `(Dec)Interval::sqrt` about 2.5 times faster on x86-64 processors without AVX-512F, by computing the square root without changing the rounding mode.
- Made `(Dec)Interval::sqrt` compute both endpoints in a single vector square root when the interval does not contain zero: about 1.8 times faster on x86-64 processors without AVX-512F, and about 1.5 times faster on WebAssembly with `simd128`.
- Made interval literals parse without GMP and MPFR, with a big integer of inari's own. That the magnitude of a number is above 2^1024 or below 2^-1074, the smallest positive `f64`, is now told from the numbers of bits of its numerator and denominator and from its exponents where these suffice, without computing its power of ten. On a Zen 2 processor, `[1e1900000000]` and `[1e-2147483648]`, whose powers of ten have over 6 × 10^9 bits, parse in less than a microsecond, where GMP and MPFR took over 20 s and 2 GB of memory. A literal with a decimal significand, or a `p` or `q` in `p/q`, of 10^5 or more digits can take longer to parse than with GMP, as inari's big integer reads and multiplies such numbers in a time that grows with the square of their number of digits: one with a significand of 10^5 digits takes about 7 ms instead of 1.6 ms, and one with 10^6 digits about 0.7 s instead of 28 ms, or 1.3 s instead of 0.23 s with the exponent `e-999999`, and `p/q` with 10^6 digits in each of `p` and `q` 1.3 s instead of 0.34 s. So can an interval literal whose bounds are close in magnitude, where one has a decimal exponent of large magnitude k and the other a hexadecimal significand of at least k bits, as inari compares them by computing 5^k exactly and multiplying by it where the numbers of bits and the exponents do not suffice: `[0x8…p-1728771, 1e-400000]`, with a hexadecimal significand of 10^5 digits, or 4 × 10^5 bits, takes about 0.13 s instead of 5.2 ms, and one with 10^6 digits and `1e-4000000` 13 s instead of 65 ms.
- Made intervals format without MPFR, with digits of inari's own, to the same text as before, as tests against MPFR check, except for the fix below. On a Zen 2 processor, formatting `Interval::PI` takes about half as long as before with `{}` and `{:.6}`, two thirds with `{:e}` and two fifths with `{:x}`.
